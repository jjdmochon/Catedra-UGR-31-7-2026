## **Section a. State-of-the-art and objectives** 

Encoded chemical libraries have been validated as an innovative way to greatly accelerate the discovery of interactions between small-molecules and biomolecules of importance such as enzymes and nucleic acids, thus becoming a technology which has attracted increasing interest from biotech and pharmaceutical companies.[1-2] These encoded strategies have been presented as a solution to screen large combinatorial libraries against biomolecules in a meaningful manner and offering a second chance to combinatorial approaches. Once, combinatorial chemistry was hailed as the future for drug discovery due to their ability of generating large number of compounds in relatively short period of time, however those great hopes were never met as they could not generate many meaningful biological data. The difficulty to screen large libraries of compounds within biological systems could not be initially overcome but encoding approaches are changing this perception. For the encoding task, DNA and Peptide Nucleic Acids (PNA) have already being used as barcode tags for a variety of applications, most notably the elegant work of David Liu in Harvard [3] and Nicolas Winssinger [4] in Strasbourg and also by myself together with Mark Bradley in Edinburgh [5]. During the past 8 years dozens of large DNA encoded small-molecules libraries have been synthesised by big institutions such as Standford, ETH Zurich and Harvard and also big pharma companies such as  GSK using split-and-mix approaches, which allow synthesising a number of compounds which increase exponentially with the number of chemical steps and therefore reducing massively time and materials required. [1, 6-8] However to date it has not been possible to test the effects of encoded small molecules on cellular phenotype in a microarray format. The latest innovation in this field has presented the use of PNA encoded peptide libraries with cellular systems such lymphocytes and adherent cell lines to find peptides as selective cellular penetrating peptides [9] and integrin ligands [10], an innovation of which I have been a key part of it. 

While those latest results show the feasibility of using these libraries with more complex systems it does still not resolve the issue of how release encoded small-molecules within cells to induce phenotypic changes and then unequivocally identified which small-molecules are responsible of those changes. In order to truly be considered a proper platform based on large library of compounds for accelerating drug discovery and also finding chemicals which induce cellular differentiation the previous issue has to be resolved for both suspension and adherent lines (e.g. stem cells, primary cells, suspension, cancer stem cells, circulating tumoral). This proposal presents key innovative steps to allow the use of large encoded libraries as chemical tools to create High-Throughput Cell-based Phenotypic Screening Platforms. 

The key question is how to cleave small-molecule compounds from their tags and still be able to track down that small-molecule after causing a phenotypic event. ChemArrays offer two different approaches depending on cell type. 

(i) _Adherent cells._ In this part, split and mix libraries having both PNA and DNA barcode tags will be synthesised on solid support, following established protocols. [1, 10] Two libraries based on triazine and adenine scaffolds will be initially prepared (see **Scheme 1** ). The key issue will be resolved by using (i) polymeric hydrogels and (ii) tailored cleavable linkers between the small-molecules and their PNA or DNA tags. The polymers in this proposal will act not just as biocompatible supports to favoured cellular attachment but also as both catalyst to allow cleavage of the tailored linkers and micro-wells to maintain small-molecules in defined places. ChemArrays will use the features of DNA/PNA to decode the libraries. This innovation will allow assessing ‘free’ small-molecule actions and not as big conjugated with their tags, DNA or PNA, which might distorsionated the real biological effect of small-molecules. These arrays will be thus base on cell-based polymer microarrays, a technology which has been exploited mainly by Prof. Langer from MIT [12] and Prof. Bradley with myself in Edinburgh [13-14] and given rise to a broad variety of applications from finding anti-bacterial materials [12] to materials which induce phagocytosis [15], hepatic

## _**(b). Synthesis of split and mix PNA-encoded libraries of small-molecules.**_ 

The synthesis will be carried out using solid phase synthesis both in parallel but also following split and mix principles. Briefly, split and mix approaches can generate a very large number of compounds which is exponential to the number of synthetic steps, therefore the number of synthetic steps grow linearly while the number of compounds generated does it exponentially. Small-molecule cores will be designed in order to give structural diversity to the libraries. Briefly, every building block will be encoded by a unique PNA unit (triplet or quadruplet, depending on the number of building blocks used). The chemistry used to build up the PNA sequence and the small-molecule compound arms will need to be fully compatible. PNA is a very robust chemical structure and there is a broad range of protecting groups this should not be a major problem. I developed new set of PNA protected monomers (Dde/Mmt) which give much more orthogonality with other chemistries[33] but other chemistries (Fmoc/Bhoc – Mmt/Boc) will be also explored.[4] 

The encoding process with PNA will be achieved as I have previously published,[33,5] thus following the addition of a building block an encoding PNA unit (monomer by monomer) will be attached in a stepwise manner before mixing and splitting. However a more elegant strategy which will be developed where PNA encoding triplets or quadruplets will be presynthesised by SPS and used as single building blocks during the synthesis of the libraries ( **Figure 6)** . This alternative will speed up the synthesis of the libraries and facilitate the increased robustness of the whole synthetic process.

## _**(d) Production of DNA-polymer hybrid arrays**_ 

Split and mix libraries of small-molecules bearing every member of the library will be hybridised onto the DNA arrays using complementary oligomers to the encoding PNA and or DNA (Fig. 3). Therefore, every member of the library will occupy a unique and spatially defined place on the 2D array. Then, polymers identified in the previous screening will be spotted either on top of every position in the array or across the whole array thereby allowing the cleavage of all the array elements and their defined embedding in the polymer substrate. The former approach will be achieved using inkjet microarraying technology that has been used to synthesise large number of unique DNA oligomers on glass slides proving the high resolution of these robots. Currently, high density DNA oligonucleotides commercialised by Agilent and OGT use a coating mask to create a very hydrophobic surface with hydrophilic islands which is where oligonucleotides are synthesised. This will help to spot hydrogels on top of DNA microarrays with a Scienion inkjet printer in a fully automatic manner. 

## _**(e) Cell-based assays using DNA-polymer arrays**_ 

Once compounds have been released, microarrays will be incubated with cell cultures, allowing the screening of 10,000 different compounds in a single experiment with different cells lines (Scheme 3). This will allow the assaying of not “just” 10,000 compounds at once but also on a variety of different cell types. Phenotypic changes induced by small-molecules will be studied by a variety of well-established methods for cellular investigation, such as immunoassays and image analysis with examination of the arrays carried out by fluorescence scanning and microscopy. 

Briefly, when cells are modified giving rise to phenotypic changes its immunology is altered producing specific antigens. Detection of these antigens by labelled antibodies has been largely used in cellular investigations. This project will extrapolate these methods to cell-based microarrays. Similar things can be said regarding pluripotent cells, well-known methods are able to detect cell differentiation and therefore, this platform will be used stem cells in order to study differentiation induced by small-molecules.

This platform will be used to induce differentiation and even a more exciting idea dedifferentiation on mammalian cells. In a very similar manner than in Objective (b), the synthesis of libraries will be done using split and mix methods in order to secure that there will be _one compound per bead_ . But in this case, compounds will permanently be attached to microspheres to be then carried into the cells. The general structure of the triazine library supported on microspheres is shown in **Figure 8** . Following protocol developed by Dr, Sanchez- Martin we will prepared fluorescently labelled microspheres and they will be used. In this case the linker will have to be cleaved intracellularly by some of the elements of the cellular machinery. 

Therefore it is forecast to use disulphide and peptides (that will be specifically cleavage by intracellular enzymes)  to act as linkers. 

_**(g) Use of the PS-microsphere supported PNA-encoded library to study phenotype changes in human immortalised myelogenous leukemia cell line K562 assisted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)**_ Following incubation of the cell model, K562 cells, with the PNA-encoded library attached to the fluorescently labelled microspheres, they will be evaluated by flow cytometry. Different parameters such as concentration and incubation time will be studied. In the first stage of the analysis, the cells will be gated based on the number of beads internalised by cells. Recently, we have observed (unpublished results that by tuning the right size ( 1.5-2 um ) of the fluorescent microspheres it is possible to gate different populations of cells depending on size. Consequently, the population of cell corresponding to one bead/one cell will be gated. This gated population will be interrogate against an specific biological event. In particular we will evaluated the cell viability/ apoptosis using Annexin/PI immunostaining. Then, populations for the assay will be sorted by FACS (FACS Aria is available at GENyO). Sorted populations will be lysated and the probe will be deconvolutated by DNA microarray or PCR amplification.
